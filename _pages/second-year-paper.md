  <div class="syp-title">Gender identity, household income composition, and the decision to work</div>
  <div class="syp-author">Allegra Saggese &mdash; UC Santa Cruz, Department of Economics</div>
  <div class="syp-abstract">Women have made substantial gains in participation in the labor market in the US, driven primarily by married women entering the workforce from the 1970s to 2000s. More recently, labor force participation growth has plateaued and women's income and work hours have persisted below men's. This research presents an argument attributing the recent stagnation in women's work as a consequence of constraints imposed by broadly accepted social norms. Men and women take on a shared identity as a couple upon marriage, and there is a penalty imposed on working couples where a wife outearns her husband (the breadwinner norm). I derive early evidence the norm varies with household wealth, and is enacted within households. The norm is difficult to detect as female labor force participation (extensive margin) has historically increased, but evidence is seen in a married woman's choice of work hours (intensive margin). Using IPUMS data from 1980 to 2024 linked to county political voting data I conduct a three-part analysis. First I replicate the Bertrand, Kamenica &amp; Pan (2015) breadwinner norm analysis to demonstrate the intensity of the norm over time. Second, I document evidence to support the norm responds to household wealth. Third, I rationalize these facts with a household model in which an identity cost of violating the breadwinner norm generates a gap between predicted and worked hours of wives. The model suggests the norm distorts labor supply, but requires further evidence of the strength of the response to household wealth. Future work warrants improved data on household wages and wealth measures.</div>
